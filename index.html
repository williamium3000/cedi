Abstract

Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have made substantial advances on benchmarks, yet their real-world effectiveness remains uncertain. This gap stems from the fundamental misalignment between benchmarks in controlled, static settings and the dynamic, interactive, and contextualized nature of real-world applications. To bridge this gap, we propose CEDI (Contextualized Evaluations of MLLMs through Dynamic, multi-round Interactions), a framework that recasts evaluation as a three-party interaction between an evaluatee model, an automated examiner, and a grader. The examiner conducts multi-turn, semi-structured conversation guided by a graph-based representation of the task. By navigating state-space transitions, CEDI deploys diverse strategies, from clarification requests to adversarial probes, to elicit performance evidence. We apply CEDI to visual hallucinations. Empirical results across multiple models, diverse settings, datasets, and domains show that contextualized, interactive evaluations reveal not only significantly more hallucinations than conventional static evaluation but also ones that more closely resemble those arising in practical use cases. We further show that hallucinations often accumulate over long contexts, through self-reinforcing dialogue history, and models are particularly vulnerable to questions requiring premise rejection or refusal. Together, these findings highlight CEDI as a step toward realistic, systematic, and ecologically valid assessments of MLLMs' capabilities.

Why evaluate through interaction?

Vision language models (VLMs) exhibit impressive abilities in visual perception, grounding, and reasoning, by adapting powerful Large Language Models (LLMs). Despite these advances, it remains unclear whether such “remarkable capabilities” can consistently transfer to realistic, real-world scenarios. A significant gap lies in the stark misalignment between existing benchmarks—typically static and conducted in controlled, laboratory settings—and the realistic usage of MLLMs, which is inherently dynamic, multi-turn, and contextualized.

To address this misalignment, we revisit MLLM evaluation as a tripartite interplay of (i) the evaluatee model, (ii) an examiner, and (iii) a grader. The examiner interacts with the evaluatee through iterative questioning, and the grader scores the resulting dialogue to produce a final performance measure.

CEDI draws inspiration from evaluative interviews in human assessment: rather than relying on static, paper-based tests, a semi-structured interview allows the examiner to adapt question type and difficulty based on the model's responses, eliciting more faithful evidence of capability. To better approximate real-world usage, CEDI introduces context modeling to operationalize the conversation over user scenarios, and leverages a graph-based representation to provide a manipulable state space for both the examiner and the grader, enabling strategies ranging from clarification requests to adversarial probes.

We apply CEDI to visual hallucination. Closely analogous to the hallucination problem in LLMs, visual hallucination refers to cases where models generate outputs that are not faithful to the image content, such as describing non-existent objects, hallucinating attributes, or fabricating incorrect relations. Existing evaluation approaches often adopt static captioning tasks in closed settings or rely on simple binary questions to assess object presence; others validate the correctness of object descriptions or generate open-ended image captions as a proxy for hallucination detection. While informative, these methods struggle to capture the nuanced, contextualized, and multi-turn nature of real-world usage where visual hallucinations arise as critical problems.

How CEDI works

CEDI recasts evaluation as a three-party interaction among an evaluatee model, an examiner, and a grader. The examiner elicits capability evidence through multi-turn interaction with the evaluatee, while the grader scores the model responses, flags potential hallucinations, and produces a final performance score. Building upon this framing, CEDI develops a contextualized, interactive examiner and a scene graph (SG)-based grader.

The examiner: a semi-structured interview over a scene graph

Drawing inspiration from interactive human assessment, we move beyond static, paper-based tests and adopt a semi-structured evaluative interview in which the examiner adapts question type and difficulty based on the model's responses. Given ground-truth (GT) visual annotations (and optionally the image), the evaluator models real-world context and conducts a multi-turn interview, dynamically adjusting its questioning strategy according to the model's prior answers.

1

Represent the image as a scene graph

For an image \(I\), the examiner stores visual content as a scene graph \(G = (V, E, \mathcal{A})\), where \(V=\{v_1,\dots,v_{|V|}\}\) are object instances, \(E \subseteq V \times \mathcal{R} \times V\) are directed, typed relation edges, and \(\mathcal{A}\) is an attribute mapping. Each node \(v\) has a category \(c(v)\in\mathcal{C}\) and attribute–value pairs \(\mathcal{A}(v) = \{(a_k, x_k)\}_{k=1}^{m_v}\), with \(a_k \in \mathcal{K}\) (e.g., color, size) and \(x_k \in \mathcal{X}_{a_k}\). Each edge \((v_i, r, v_j)\) represents a predicate \(r(v_i, v_j)\) (e.g., on, holding, next to).

This yields a manipulable state space for both examiner and grader: questions are grounded in faithful visual content rather than driven solely by language priors.

2

Model a user's context

Users interact with MLLMs from a first-person scenario that combines context with an explicit objective (“I am visually impaired and want to locate the restroom in this restaurant,” or “I am driving in a park and need to find a parking lot.”). The examiner treats \(I\) as the user's immediate environment and constructs a scenario \(s\) with goal \(g\) grounded in \(G\); the goal requires reasoning over multiple elements rather than single-object recognition.

In parallel, it selects a context-relevant node subset \(V_s \subseteq V\), forming a sub-graph \(G_s = (V_s, E_s, \mathcal{A}_s)\) with \(E_s = \{(v_i, r, v_j) \in E : v_i, v_j \in V_s\}\) and \(\mathcal{A}_s = \mathcal{A}|_{V_s}\).

3

Converse by traversing the sub-graph

At turn \(t\), the examiner randomly selects a target node \(v_t \in V_s\) (or retains \(v_{t-1}\) for a follow-up) and generates a question grounded in \(v_t\) and its local structure. Three properties matter: (i) grounded traversal — every turn is anchored to a concrete node, so questions stay verifiable against \(G\); (ii) goal-driven interaction — \(s\) and \(g\) persist across turns, making the dialogue purposeful rather than a sequence of independent queries; (iii) contextual continuity, follow-up consistency, and conversational dependency — conditioning every turn on the prior conversation stress-tests whether later answers stay consistent with earlier claims.

Four question types

From this scaffold, the examiner actively selects one of four question types to elicit different hallucination behaviors — adversarial questions are only one component of this repertoire. Type selection is conditioned on the conversation history and previously used types to avoid repetitive questions and support a coherent dialogue flow.

regularEstablish a factual baselineGrounded in \(v_t\) and the goal \(g\), querying observable objects, attributes, or relations in \(G_s\) under realistic user objectives.
follow-upTest consistencyConditions on the dialogue history and the model's prior claims to confirm, clarify, or interrogate earlier responses, keeping \(v_t = v_{t-1}\).
adversarialExploit co-occurrence priorsAsks about a highly plausible but absent object \(v \notin V_s\), attribute \((a,x) \notin \mathcal{A}_s(v_t)\), or relation \((v_i,r,v_j)\notin E_s\) that commonly co-occurs with the visual content.
unanswerableInject a false premiseFabricates an unsupported element \(p\), then composes a dependent query \(q(p)\) whose answer is undefined: \(G \not\models p \Rightarrow G \not\models q(p)\).
Example. Given a scene graph encoding a cake on a table and a fork nearby, the examiner may fabricate a node \(v_{\text{man}}\notin V_s\) and a relation \((v_{\text{man}},\texttt{holding},v_{\text{fork}})\notin E_s\), then ask: “What is the man using to eat the cake?” A well-grounded model should refuse or correct the premise (“There is no man in the image”), while hallucinating models tend to fabricate (“the man is holding the fork to eat the cake”).

Examiner backbone. The examiner is empowered by an MLLM such as GPT-4o. We prompt it to generate the scenario and objectives, actively select the question type for each turn, and generate diverse yet contextually and visually grounded questions.

Scaling contexts for coverage. Beyond precision, recall (coverage of image content) is critical: otherwise a model can minimize risk by producing overly sparse descriptions, remaining correct simply by saying little. Enforcing exhaustive coverage is misaligned with a brief caption and incompatible with single-turn VQA. We argue that coverage should be driven by the examiner, not imposed by the grader: the examiner generates diverse, goal-driven contexts, each selecting a different sub-graph \(G_s\). As the number of sampled contexts grows, the union of queried sub-graphs expands, yielding higher coverage while preserving naturalistic interaction.

The grader: compare scene graphs, not strings

The grader parses the MLLM's transcript into a predicted scene graph and compares it against the ground-truth one. Discrepancies correspond to either hallucinated (less faithful) or missing (less covered) objects, attributes, and relations.

Let the GT scene graph be \(G^{\star}=(V^{\star},E^{\star},\mathcal{A}^{\star})\) and the transcript over \(T\) turns be \(\mathcal{T}=\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{T}\). An LLM-based parser extracts semantic tuples at the sentence level and aggregates them into a predicted graph \(\hat{G}=(\hat{V},\hat{E},\hat{\mathcal{A}})\); candidate entities are restricted to WordNet physical-entity classes to filter non-physical nouns.

Graph Edit Distance (GED) computes the minimum total cost of edit operations that transform one graph into another:

$$\mathrm{GED}(\hat{G},G^{\star})= \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\hat{G},G^{\star})} \sum_{e \in \pi} c(e)$$

Insertions penalize missing visual content (in \(G^{\star}\) but not \(\hat{G}\)), whereas deletions penalize hallucinated entities or relations (in \(\hat{G}\) but not \(G^{\star}\)), so GED jointly captures hallucination and coverage.

SG ΔCon compares node-affinity matrices computed with fast belief propagation, using the Matusita distance between propagated influence patterns, plus a normalized symmetric difference on attributes:

$$d_{\Delta}(\hat{G},G^{\star})=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}\Big(\sqrt{S_{\hat{G},ij}}-\sqrt{S_{G^{\star},ij}}\Big)^2},\qquad d_{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{|\hat{\mathcal{A}}\,\triangle\,\mathcal{A}^{\star}|}{|\hat{\mathcal{A}}\cup\mathcal{A}^{\star}|},\qquad d=d_{\Delta}+\lambda d_{\mathcal{A}}$$

Experimental setup

  • Datasets. 100 images from human-annotated Visual Genome (VG), whose dense object, attribute, relationship, and region annotations are converted into GT scene graphs; and 500 images from Synthetic Visual Genome (SVG) (100 VG, 200 ADE-20K, 200 COCO) to assess cross-domain generalization.
  • Evaluatees. Open-source: LLaVA-1.5-7B (and its OPERA-decoding variant, designed to mitigate hallucinations), InternVL2-8B, InternVL2.5-8B, InternVL3-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, Gemma-3-12B-it. Proprietary: GPT-4o and GPT-5.4 mini.
  • Baseline examiners. (i) the caption-based examiner (“brief description”), (ii) the binary-question examiner (POPE), and (iii) our prompt-only baseline, which follows pre-defined conversations as in-context examples to elicit hallucinations via contextualized, multi-turn interactions.
  • Graders. CHAIRS/CHAIRI and object coverage; MMHal (GPT-4-based, 0–6 severity); HaELM (LLaMA-based detector); VALOR faithi and covi; and our SG-based GED and SG ΔCon. Full definitions are in the details below.
  • Human annotation. Crowdsourced annotators mark phrase-level hallucinations (object existence and counts, attributes, relationships, scene text), aggregated into sample- and sentence-level scores.

We organize experiments around three research questions: RQ1 Do contextualized, multi-turn, and dynamic interactions reveal more VLM hallucinations than conventional static evaluation? RQ2 Does the SG-based CEDI grader provide a more consistent and human-aligned assessment? RQ3 What behavioral insights does CEDI offer for evaluating the hallucinations of VLMs?

…and the conversations look like real use

For realism, annotators rank complete conversations on 50 sampled images by how closely they resemble natural VLM usage. CEDI is consistently preferred over Caption and POPE, with a statistically significant improvement over POPE (\(p=.011\)). For relevance, annotators judge 100 question–answer pairs: is each generated question, or each elicited hallucination, directly relevant to the user's stated objective? For a fair comparison, Caption is re-run with the same context description as CEDI and POPE questions are regenerated from the GT annotation and the context. CEDI achieves higher relevance for both, while the baselines more often surface errors involving objects or relations peripheral to the context.

RQ2 · Analysis of the graderA scene-graph grader that tracks humans

Human annotators flag hallucination (precision) but not coverage (recall), so overall GED is not directly comparable to human judgments. To disentangle the two failure modes, we define two GED variants anchored on the synset-aligned intersection \(G^{\cap}=\hat{G}\cap G^{\star}\):

$$\mathrm{GED}_{\text{hal}}=\mathrm{GED}(G^{\cap},\hat{G}), \qquad \mathrm{GED}_{\text{cov}}=\mathrm{GED}(G^{\cap},\hat{G}\cup G^{\star})$$

\(\mathrm{GED}_{\text{hal}}\) isolates prediction-only content (precision, aligned with human annotations); \(\mathrm{GED}_{\text{cov}}\) captures both hallucinated and uncovered content. We compute the Pearson correlation \(\rho\) between each grader score and the human-annotated hallucination count across 50 images per evaluatee.

Refusal-demanding questions break models most

Per-turn dynamics are measured with VALOR \(1-\mathrm{faith}_i\), the fraction of object mentions at turn \(i\) that match no ground-truth object (the SG-based grader evaluates the accumulated transcript and is unsuitable or costly per turn). Regular, follow-up, and adversarial questions account for most hallucinated turns; although unanswerable questions occur less frequently, they yield the highest per-turn hallucination rate. Overall, refusal-demanding questions (adversarial and unanswerable) are more error-prone than fact-seeking ones, particularly when the model can condition on its prior responses.

Hallucinations accumulate as the conversation goes on

Without conversation history, the hallucination rate remains low and nearly flat (21–25% after the opening turns). With history, it begins near 26%, rises to roughly 30–32% within the first third of the conversation, and then plateaus around 30%. Long contexts increase VLMs' susceptibility to visual hallucination, potentially because attention to the visual input gradually weakens as the dialogue progresses.

What the errors look like

Human-annotated errors are dominated by hallucinated objects (62%), particularly in unanswerable and adversarial turns. Wrong attributes account for 20% and frequently arise in follow-up turns, where models revise previously correct attributes under conversational pressure. Wrong relations contribute another 15%, typically asserting an incorrect relation for an existing object.

Conclusion

We introduced CEDI, a contextualized evaluation framework that recasts VLM assessment as a three-party interaction among an evaluatee, a dynamic examiner, and a grader. Guided by scene graphs, the examiner conducts context-driven, multi-turn interaction using regular, follow-up, adversarial, and unanswerable questions, while the SG-based grader evaluates hallucinations beyond object-level errors by capturing incorrect objects, attributes, and relations, together with coverage. Across multiple models, datasets, automatic metrics, and human annotations, CEDI consistently exposes more hallucinations, achieves broader visual coverage, and produces interactions and failures that are more relevant to realistic user contexts than captioning, binary-question, and prompt-only baselines. Our analyses further show that contextualization, conversational history, and multi-round interaction are central to this effect: hallucinations often accumulate through self-reinforcing dialogue history, and models are particularly vulnerable to questions requiring premise rejection or refusal. Although the current instantiation focuses on visual hallucination, we argue that the underlying formulation and evaluation framework are general and can be extended to broader tasks and applications. We hope CEDI motivates a broader shift from static benchmark testing toward adaptive, context-dependent evaluation of multimodal systems under realistic interaction.

Limitations

  • Dependence on ground-truth scene graphs. To ground the examiner in faithful visual content and enable structured scoring, we primarily use annotated scene graphs, preventing the examiner from relying on language priors alone. To reduce this dependency, we validate CEDI on SVG, whose scene graphs are automatically constructed from diverse sources rather than hand-labeled.
  • Examiner backbone accessibility. We use GPT-4o as the default examiner backbone for its strong instruction-following and context-modeling capabilities. Both the examiner and the grader are instantiated with LLM/MLLM components, so their quality is bounded by the underlying backbone models.
  • Transcript-level grading. The SG-based grader assesses the accumulated, transcript-level response; it is ill-suited (or prohibitively costly) for grading individual turns, for which we rely on per-turn signals such as VALOR.

More details

Related work

Static evaluation. Static benchmarks remain the dominant paradigm for MLLM evaluation, spanning VQA, image captioning, OCR/text understanding, and more recently core cognitive abilities and higher-level reasoning such as MathVerse and ScienceQA. GQA, OK-VQA, OwlEval, MMBench, and MMMU evaluate visual perception and reasoning through large collections of question–answer pairs; SEED-Bench and SEED-Bench-2-Plus extend evaluation to video and text modalities. Despite their breadth, static benchmarks increasingly suffer from performance saturation and data contamination, and more fundamentally ignore the dynamic, interactive, and contextualized nature of real-world MLLM use.

Dynamic evaluation. Recent studies evaluate MLLMs in interactive, conversational settings. MMDU targets multi-turn dialogues over multiple images; ConvBench organizes interaction sequences to progressively probe perception, reasoning, and creativity; MMRC assesses open-ended conversational capabilities. These efforts remain constrained by static rubrics and largely pre-scripted conversation structures, and offer limited mechanisms for explicit context modeling.

Visual hallucination and its evaluation. VLMs may generate plausible but non-existent objects, attributes, or relations, often driven by spurious co-occurrence patterns learned during training rather than visual evidence — undermining reliability in critical applications such as autonomous driving. Evaluation spans frameworks, benchmarks, grading methods, and datasets for training hallucination detectors, but most approaches are single-turn (e.g., caption-based) and rely on coarse binary labels (e.g., yes/no object presence), poorly reflecting multi-turn, context-dependent interactions and rarely differentiating hallucination severity under varying contexts.

Contextualized Caption and POPE baselines (relevance study)

Caption. The scenario is prepended as a plain instruction: “Considering this context: {context}, please provide a detailed description.”, where {context} is the same background + goal string CEDI receives.

POPE. The yes/no pool is regenerated per image from the scenario and scene graph, with the number of questions matched to the number of CEDI rounds on that image:

  1. Candidate pool. Given {background, goal}, Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 lists \(N=50\) concrete visible objects that could appear in the scenario, mixing plausible-present and plausible-absent.
  2. Existence + co-occurrence check. Given the GT object list and the candidates, the LLM labels each yes/no using synonym and parent-class matching, and flags which no-candidates are frequent real-world co-occurrences (plausible distractors).
  3. Composition. Draw \(n_{\text{rounds}}\) questions in the ratio \(\tfrac12\) yes : \(\tfrac14\) random no : \(\tfrac14\) co-occurrence no, formatted as “is there a ⟨object⟩ in the image?”
Human annotation guidelines
Hallucination annotation
Your task is to assess whether the content in the conversation conflicts with the image. Identify the segment (or sub-sentence) that is wrong / conflicting / irrelevant regarding the image, given several captions and objects with locations. Pay attention to:
  1. Object: whether objects exist and whether their quantity conflicts with the object information.
  2. Attributes: whether color, position, or action conflicts with the attribute information.
  3. Relations: whether the relation between objects and their interaction is correct.
  4. Scene Text: whether text mentioned in the conversation matches the text in the image.
Conversation realism ranking
Read three conversations between a person and an AI about the same image. Rank them by how well they reflect the way real people actually use AI assistants — not whether the language sounds natural, but whether this is the kind of exchange someone would genuinely have with an AI. 1 Most realistic: exactly the kinds of questions someone would type in everyday use. 2 Moderately realistic. 3 Least realistic: feels like a benchmark test or academic exercise. Use each rank exactly once; ignore whether the AI's answers are correct.
Conversation relevance
Given the background and goal, tick each question Q# and each highlighted answer snippet H# that is related to the task's goal. Rule of thumb: an item is related to the goal if a person trying to accomplish the goal would care whether it is correct.
Ethics statement

This work aims to advance the evaluation of VLMs through a contextualized and interactive framework for diagnosing hallucinations, with potential positive impact on real-world deployments such as assistive tools, where fabricated visual information may lead to user confusion or safety risks. Any automated evaluator can itself be imperfect and should not be the sole basis for deployment decisions. All images come from publicly available datasets (VG, COCO, ADE20K, and the SVG collection) under their respective licenses, and no personally identifiable information was collected. Crowdsourced annotators were compensated for their time; all model-generated content was reviewed for sensitive or harmful material before being shown to annotators.
